Career

Badger Meter (Dec 2018 - Nov 2025) +

In September of 2018, my friend Kiarash Mavandad, who I met at Berkeley, told me about an opening at his company for a software engineering role. He was a Computer Science major with programming internships in college and a couple years of software experience under his belt. As an applied math major with no tech experience I felt unqualified for such a role, but the prospect of working reduced hours for double pay while acquiring valuable skills was appealing. Programming had been my favorite part of all the previous jobs I had so it felt like a great fit. He put in a high recommendation for me, campaigning his managers to give me a shot, and managed to get me an interview. I knew nothing about software engineering and scrambled over the next several days to cover a list of technologies he sent me.

I went into the interview and it didn't go well. I found myself answering "no" to almost every software-related question they asked. The interview was brief, as it was clear I didn't have what they were looking for, but for final questions I made a plea. I told them I really enjoyed programming and I would work as hard as I could for reduced pay if they gave me a chance. Unfortunately I was rejected, but the head manager, Shahram Javey, appreciated my passion and reached out with some advice on what to learn. I took notes and began working on projects every day after work with the goal of switching to software engineering once I built the skillset.

About one month later, Shahram made a surprising call to check in. I excitedly told him about the projects I had been working on; I had built a chess engine in JavaScript from scratch and a CRM with Python and React. He wanted to see the code, and after reviewing the projects, he called back to say the team wanted to re-interview me. I was delighted. I went in for my second interview, and after showing marked improvement, they offered me a three month contract. I couldn't have been happier.

I went in for the new job and it was clear I had a long way to go. The other engineers would say things to me using technical jargon that I didn't understand, and I would write down notes to review later. I worked long hours, struggling to wrap my head around the dozens of technologies and hundreds of technical terms I needed to know. My manager would call me in and show me complex DSL queries written against an Elasticsearch cluster, and I watched quietly wondering how I could ever make sense of any of it.

I did what I could to piece together some understanding, and when I was assigned my first feature I poured over the code base trying as hard as I could to grasp it. For almost a month I worked late every day. I poured over thousands of lines of code and integrated the feature. When everything seemed to be working, I tentatively submitted my code for review, expecting it to be shredded. To my surprise, a senior engineer left a glowing review, and even encouraged my manager to extend my contract to full time. For the first time I felt like I belonged.

For the next couple years, I continued to build my skill set and take on larger projects. The gaps in my understanding were growing fewer and further between, and the managers began to trust me with greater responsibility. I became one of only a few administrators on our cloud infrastructure platform and was permitted to build services and design my own systems independtly. I took control of our Elasticsearch cluster to implement cost saving strategies and became proficient enough at the query DSL to help out the engineers who had taught me. I was promoted to Senior Software Engineer and was now frequently managing other engineers and consulting on projects.

I greatly enjoyed the freedom of my role and loved working under Shahram, the manager who had encouraged me from the start. I later found out that he was the only one who wanted to hire me after my first interview. A PhD Computer Scientist and Iranian immigrant with a long tenure at Adobe, he had founded a startup utilizing cellular devices to broadcast water meter data to cloud servers, which was acquired by Badger Meter. Through the acquisition, he retained his role as head of software and kept an office in Los Gatos, far from the corporate headquarters in Milwaukee. He valued creativity and had a true love of programming. He cared deeply about the fulfillment and well being of his employees, shouldering corporate responsibility and advocating for promotions on behalf of his engineers. He had a great sense of humor and was kind and forgiving. He believed in people and wanted to give everyone a shot. When the building janitor came up and asked if his son could have a summer internship, Shahram didn't hesistate, "Asbsolutley", he said. The janitors son ended up being a fantastic dev ops engineer. Though in his mid sixties, Shahram was constantly reading up on the latest tech material and would excitedly call us in to demo something new. I looked up to him and will forever be grateful for everything he did for me.

In 2024 I went to Milwaukee for a work assignment and unfortunately got re-orged to a newly formed team. Our company had recently acquired several other IoT technology companies and wanted to embark on a project to unify all the software. They put our best front end engineer on the team and wanted me to drive the backend development. The project was bold and half-baked. I would receive hundred-page PDFs of APIs that had to be incorporated and GitHub repositories with vastly different backend stacks. The design was incomplete, and the vision for the app was always changing. My new manager had only been at the company a year and was caught between engineers pushing back on the feasibility of the project and executives telling him to get it done.

I deliberated burning the midnight oil to attempt to get the project through, but I didn't believe in the vision and knew the effort would be Herculean. At age 30, I didn't have the same energy I had at 25, when I would wander down to 7-11 and load up on redbull for a night of coding. I explained my concerns to my manager and asked if I could be moved to a different team, but ultimately I was told the project was a priority. A couple months later, I put in my notice.

My time at Badger was a fantastic experience, and I am incredibly grateful. I learned so much and loved my time in San Jose, Campbell, and Los Gatos. The office was right below the Santa Cruz Mountains, and there was nothing like biking up the mountains to get some exercise and catch a sunset after work. I remain in touch with many former coworkers and may even return some day.

Southern Glazers Wine and Spirits (Sep 2017 - Dec 2018) +

I got my first full time job working as a data analyst at Southern Glazer's Wine and Spirits. I had my Applied Math degree, but what actually got me the role was my experience in VBA. A programmer had left, and they needed someone to fill in to support the VBA programs he built. Many of the programs queried data from an Oracle SQL server, and I began to learn how to build SQL queries to support data feeds. I really enjoyed SQL, and became one of the goto resources in the office for data needs. I automated reports and started to incorporate python scripting into workflows to automate operational tasks. I created my own SQL DBs to normalize and make accessible messy data. Pretty soon I realized that what I enjoyed most about my job was not the data analysis, but the programming.

Delta Investment Management (2014 - 2015) +

My first job was an internship at Delta Investment Management, a registered investment advisor. I was exposed to programming for the first time, assigned with the task of modifying a VBA macro that simulated a trading strategy. After some experimentation and plenty of googling, the code began to make sense, and I ended up automating data retrieval, trading strategy backtesting, performance metric calculators, and several other operational tasks for the firm.

Personal

  • Chess +

    I began playing chess online on chess.com in 2014 while in college. I started around a 600 rating (beginner level) but progressed quickly. By mid 2015 I reached a rating of 2000 and was in the 99th percentile of players on the site. I was in the 2200 rating range by the time I graduated.

    In late 2019, I got a new job as a software engineer and happened to end up living less than a mile away from the Kolty Chess Club in Campbell. I started playing the weekly tournaments to establishing an official rating, with the hopes of one day achieving the National Master title. The goal was daunting and unlikely. To get there I was going to need a national rating of 2200, higher than 99% of tournament players and above anyone at the club.

    After a couple tournaments, my first established US Chess rating was 2006 and it seemed like the goal was in the realm of possibility. After a two year COVID closure, in 2022 I was back in the club playing 3 hour games on the Wednesday night meetups. In 2023 I crossed 2100 and won the club championship. In 2024 I became the highest rated player at the club and began venturing to the Mechanics Institute in San Francisco to face stronger competition. Twice I came within ten rating points of the title only to fall back down in rating. It felt like I couldn't quite break through and I began to question my ability to make it as well as all the time I was putting in.

    I finally got the break I needed in early 2025, when two good tournaments propelled me to 2200. I was officially a master and was relieved that I could now dial it back and play for fun.

    Through 2025 and 2026, I played chess but much less intensely, mostly playing speed games online instead of long tournament games. My online chess.com speed chess rating reached a peak of 2619 in 2026, which was good enough to put me in the 99.99th percentile! I'm still active online, and playing a couple big tournaments every year, but not with the same drive I had when I was going for the title. My official ratings have fallen and it seems unlikely I'll be able to get them back up, but I really enjoy playing stress free.

    Feel free to add me on chess.com if you have an account.
