Career

Badger Meter (Dec 2018 - Nov 2025) +

In September of 2018, my friend Kiarash Mavandad, who I had met at Berkeley, told me about an opening at his company for a software engineering role. He was a Computer Science major with programming internships in college and a couple of years of software experience under his belt. As an Applied Math major with no professional tech experience, I felt unqualified for such a role, but the prospect of working fewer hours for double the pay while acquiring valuable skills was appealing. Not to mention, programming had been my favorite part of all my previous jobs. Kiarash campaigned on my behalf, trying to convince them to give me a chance, and managed to get me an interview. I knew almost nothing about software engineering and spent the next few days scrambling to learn some basics.

I went into the interview, and it didn't go well. I found myself answering "no" to almost every software related question they asked. The interview was brief, as it quickly became clear that I didn't have the experience they were looking for. When they asked if I had any final questions, I made one last plea. I told them how much I enjoyed programming and that I could work for reduced pay if they were willing to give me a chance. Unfortunately I was rejected. However, the head manager, Shahram Javey, appreciated my enthusiasm and reached out afterward with advice on what I should learn. I took notes and began working on projects every day after work with the goal of transitioning into software engineering.

About one month later, Shahram made a surprising call to check in. I excitedly told him about the projects I had been working on; I had built a chess engine in JavaScript from scratch and a CRM with Python and React. He wanted to see the code, and after reviewing the projects, he called back to say the team wanted to re-interview me. I was delighted. I went in for my second interview, and after showing marked improvement, they offered me a three month contract. I couldn't have been happier.

I went in for the new job and it was clear I had a long way to go. The other engineers would say things to me using technical jargon that I didn't understand, and I would take down pages of notes to review later. I worked long hours, struggling to wrap my head around the dozens of technologies and hundreds of technical terms I needed to know. My manager would call me in and show me complex DSL queries written against an Elasticsearch cluster, and I watched quietly wondering how I could ever make sense of any of it.

I did what I could to piece together an understanding, and when I was assigned my first feature, I dove into the code base, trying as hard as I could to grasp it. For almost a month, I worked late every day. I pored over thousands of lines of code and eventually integrated the feature. When everything seemed to be working, I tentatively submitted my code for review, expecting it to be shredded. To my surprise, a senior engineer left a glowing review and encouraged my manager to offer me a full-time position. I made it!

For the next couple of years, I continued to build my skill set and take on larger projects. The gaps in my understanding grew fewer and farther between, and management began to trust me with greater responsibility. I became one of only a few administrators on our cloud infrastructure platform and was given the freedom to build services and design systems independently. I took control of our Elasticsearch cluster, implementing cost-saving strategies and becoming proficient enough with the query DSL to become the division's leading resource on Elasticsearch. I was promoted to Senior Software Engineer and frequently found myself managing other engineers and consulting with other teams on projects.

My primary work centered around processing, analyzing, and storing data received from more than ten million IoT devices transmitting minutely sensor readings. I developed parallelized systems capable of scaling to thousands of concurrent workers to ingest, normalize, and store this data in Elasticsearch clusters. I worked on APIs that provided data access through a custom JSON-based query language capable of being translated into SQL queries, Elasticsearch DSL, and in memory filtering against Python dataclasses. The language was built around three core components: a field, an operator, and a value. It proved flexible enough to support everything from simple text matching to geospatial polygon filtering and was eventually adopted across nearly all of our APIs. Another major initiative was the development of a rules engine that allowed customers to define filters against incoming sensor streams to generate notifications and detect issues in near real time. The feature was highly successful and became a major selling point of the platform. I wrote thousands of automated tests to protect the functionality of these systems and regularly led test driven development training sessions for the engineering team.

I greatly enjoyed the freedom of my role and loved working under Shahram, the manager who had encouraged me from the start. I later found out that he had been the only person who wanted to hire me after my first interview. A PhD computer scientist and Iranian immigrant with a long tenure at Adobe, he had founded a startup that used cellular devices to transmit water meter data to cloud servers, which was later acquired by Badger Meter. Through the acquisition, he retained his role as head of software and kept an office in Los Gatos, far from the corporate headquarters in Milwaukee. He valued creativity and had a genuine love of programming. He cared deeply about the fulfillment and well-being of his employees, shouldering corporate responsibilities and advocating for promotions on behalf of his engineers. He had a great sense of humor and was kind and forgiving. Most importantly, he believed in people and wanted to give everyone a chance. One day, the building janitor approached him and asked whether his son could have a summer internship. Shahram didn't hesitate. "Absolutely," he said. The janitor's son went on to become an excellent DevOps engineer. Though he was in his mid-sixties, Shahram was constantly reading about the latest technologies and would excitedly call us into his office to demonstrate something new he had discovered. I look up to him as a role model and will forever be grateful for what he did for me.

In 2024, I went to Milwaukee for a work assignment and was unfortunately re-orged to a newly formed team. Our company had recently acquired several other IoT technology companies and wanted to embark on a project to unify all of the software. They pulled our best front-end engineer onto the team and put me in charge of backend development. The project was bold and half-baked. I was handed hundred-page API specifications that had to be incorporated, along with GitHub repositories built on vastly different technology stacks. The design was incomplete, and the vision for the application was constantly changing. My new manager had only been at the company for a year and was caught between engineers pushing back on the feasibility of the project and executives pressuring him to get it done.

I deliberated burning the midnight oil, but I didn't believe in the path we were on and knew the effort required would be Herculean. There were more important things I felt I could be working on, and I hoped I could persuade my manager to let me switch projects. I explained my concerns to my manager and asked if I could be moved to a different team, but ultimately I was told the project was the company's top priority. A couple of months later, I put in my notice.

My time at Badger was a fantastic experience, and I am incredibly grateful. I learned so much and loved my time in San Jose, Campbell, and Los Gatos. I remain in touch with many former coworkers, including Shahram, and may return someday.

I found that I enjoyed working with SQL and became quite proficient at querying. I automated reports, built data feeds, and incorporated Python scripts into business processes. Before long, I realized that what I enjoyed most wasn't the data analysis, but the programming around it.

Personal

Chess +

I began playing chess online on chess.com in 2014 while in college. I started around a 600 rating (beginner level) but progressed quickly. By mid 2015 I reached a rating of 2000 (advanced level). By the time I graduated I was a skilled speed chess player with a 2200 online rating.

In late 2019, I started a new job as a software engineer and ended up moving less than a mile from the Kolty Chess Club in Campbell. I began playing in weekly tournaments to establish an official rating, with the dream of one day earning the National Master title. The challenge was daunting, and I wasn't sure I could do it. To earn the title, I would need to reach a US Chess rating of 2200, higher than 99% of tournament players and anyone at my local club. The appeal was a lifelong title, and the opportunity to compete in prize-money events against some of the best players in the world. I had always held the goal as a pipe dream but now there was a real path to get there.

After a couple tournaments, my established US Chess rating was 2006 and it seemed like there was hope. A two year COVID closure put a hold on things, but in 2022 I was back in the club playing the weekly tournament games. My rating began to climb to the upper 2000s, and in 2023 I crossed 2150 and won the club championship. In 2024 I began venturing to the Mechanics Institute in San Francisco to face better opposition. The top players in SF were all masters, and it was tough adjusting to the level of play. My results were volatile as I bounced between 2150 and 2190, never quite reaching the threshold. In one tournament I was within 6 points of the title, only to draw a critical game and fall back down. It felt like I couldn't quite break through and I was beginning to question if I could make it.

I finally got the break I needed in early 2025, when two strong tournament performances and a win against a young master propelled me to a rating of 2200. I was officially a National Master. Oddly enough, I felt more relief than joy. The pursuit of the title had been stressful. As my rating climbed, the risk reward balance became increasingly unforgiving. A win might earn me two rating points, while a draw could cost ten and a loss twenty. A single bad game could erase months of progress.

On one occasion, I was completely lost and only moments away from a twenty-point hit. I had taken the train an hour and a half into San Francisco, riding a bike to the club, and would not get home until past midnight after the four-hour game, with work to do before morning. My stomach dropped as I watched my opponent reach for what I was certain would be the winning move. By some miracle, he missed it. The game turned around, and I eventually won. When I walked out of the playing hall, I was drenched in sweat, pale, and nauseous. The game had literally made me sick.

Through the remainder of 2025 and 2026, I continued playing chess, but much less intensely, focusing primarily on speed chess online. Motivated by some success, I briefly pushed my rating to anew high over 2600, though this is likely as far as I will ever go. I participated in a handful of prize-money events and enjoyed playing against strong masters, but my tournament activity has been limited by schedules that often conflict with work hours. Although I still enjoy playing, I don't have much reason to keep pushing for progress, and I will likely focus other things.

Feel free to add me on chess.com if you have an account.
