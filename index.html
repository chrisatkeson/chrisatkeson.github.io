Career

Badger Meter (Dec 2018 - Nov 2025)

In September of 2018, my friend Kiarash Mavandad, who I met at Berkeley, told me about an opening at his company for a software engineering role. He was a Computer Science major with programming internships in college and a couple years of software experience under his belt. I felt unqualified for such a role, but he assured me I could do it, and the prospect of working tech hours for double pay while acquiring marketable skills was something I had to try. He put in a high recommendation for me, campaigning his managers to give me a shot, and somehow managed to get me an interview. I knew nothing about modern software design and scrambled over the next several days to get some kind of understanding, but I knew it was going to be a long shot.

I went into the interview and proceeded to answer "no" to almost every software-related question they asked. The interview was brief, and it was clear I didn't have the experience they wanted, but for final questions I made a plea. I told them I really enjoyed programming and that I would work as hard as I could for reduced pay to get up to speed.

Unfortunately it didn't work, but the head manager, Shahram Javey, appreciated my passion and reached out with some advice on what to learn. I took notes and began working on projects every day after work with the goal of switching to software.

About one month later, the manager made a surprising call to check in. I excitedly updated him on the projects I had been working on; I had built a chess engine in JavaScript from scratch and a CRM with Python and React. He wanted to see the code, and after reviewing the projects, he called back to say the team wanted to re-interview me. I was delighted. I went in for my second interview, and after showing marked improvement, they offered me a three month contract. I couldn't have been happier.

I went in for the new job and it was clear I had a long way to go. The other engineers would say things to me in technical jargon that was almost unintelligible, and I would write down notes to review later to quietly learn. I worked long hours, struggling to wrap my head around the dozens of technologies and hundreds of technical terms I needed to know. My manager would call me in and show me complex DSL queries written against an Elasticsearch cluster, and I watched quietly wondering how I could ever make sense of such a mess. I did what I could to piece together some understanding, and when I was assigned my first feature I poured over the code base trying as hard as I could to grasp it. For almost a month I worked late with the sole focus of executing on my project. I poured over thousands of lines of code and integrated a feature that had to be woven into a fragile part of the code base. When everything seemed to be working, I tentatively submitted my code review, expecting to be shredded. To my surprise, the senior engineer left a glowing review, and encouraged my manager to extend my contract to full time. For the first time I felt like I belonged.

For the next couple years, I continued to build my skill set and take on larger projects. The gaps in my understanding were growing fewer and further between, and the managers began to trust me with greater responsibility. I became one of only a couple administrators on our cloud infrastructure platform and was allowed to build my own services and design my own systems. I took control of our Elasticsearch cluster to implement cost saving strategies and became proficient enough at the query DSL to help out the engineers who had taught me. I was promoted to Senior Software Engineer and was now frequently managing other engineers and consulting on projects.

I greatly enjoyed the freedom of my role and loved working under Shahram, the senior manager who had believed in me in the first place. A PhD Computer Scientist and Iranian immigrant with a long tenure at Adobe, he had founded a startup utilizing cellular devices to broadcast water meter readings to cloud servers, which was acquired by Badger Meter. Through the acquisition, he retained his role as head of software and kept an office in Los Gatos, far from the corporate headquarters in Milwaukee. He valued creativity and had a true love of programming. He cared most about the fulfillment and well being of those under him, shouldering a lot of the corporate responsibility to free up his engineers. He had a great sense of humor and was kind and forgiving. Though in his mid sixties, he was constantly reading up on the latest tech material and would excitedly call us in when he learned something new. I looked up to him and will forever be grateful for everything he did for me.

In 2024 I went to Milwaukee for a work assignment and unfortunately got permanently re-orged to a newly formed team. Our company had recently acquired several other IoT technology companies and wanted to embark on a project to unify all the software. They put our best front end engineer on the team and wanted me to drive the backend development. The project was bold and half-baked. I would receive hundred-page PDFs of APIs that had to be incorporated and GitHub repositories with vastly different backend stacks. The design was incomplete, and the vision for the app was always changing. My new manager had only been at the company a year and was caught between engineers pushing back on the reality of the project and higher-level managers and executives telling him to get it done.

I deliberated burning the midnight oil to attempt to get the project through, but I didn't believe in the vision and knew the effort would be Herculean. I explained my concerns to my manager and asked if I could be moved to a different team, but ultimately I was told the project was the highest priority. A couple months later, I put in my notice.

My time at Badger was a fantastic experience, and I am incredibly grateful. I learned so much and lived some of my best years in San Jose, Campbell, and Los Gatos. I remain in touch with many former coworkers and may even return some day.

Southern Glazers Wine and Spirits (Sep 2017 - Dec 2018)

I got my first full time job working as a data analyst at Southern Glazer's Wine and Spirits. I had my Applied Math degree, but what actually got me the role was my experience in VBA. A programmer had left, and they needed someone to fill in to support the VBA programs he built. Many of the programs queried data from an Oracle SQL server, and I began to learn how to build SQL queries to support data feeds. I really enjoyed SQL, and became one of the goto resources in the office for data needs. I automated reports and started to incorporate python scripting into workflows to automate operational tasks. I created my own SQL DBs to normalize and make accessible messy data. Pretty soon I realized that what I enjoyed most about my job was not the data analysis, but the programming.

Delta Investment Management (2014 - 2015)

My first job was an internship at Delta Investment Management, a registered investment advisor. I was exposed to programming for the first time, assigned with the task of modifying a VBA macro that simulated a trading strategy. After some experimentation and plenty of googling, the code began to make sense, and I ended up automating data retrieval, trading strategy backtesting, performance metric calculators, and several other operational tasks for the firm.
